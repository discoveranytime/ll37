LL-37
the body’s antibiotic

Your skin and immune cells manufacture their own antibiotic — a 37-link peptide that shreds microbes and directs wound repair. Scientists are trying to turn it into a drug. Here is where that stands.

Quick read

LL-37 is the only human member of the cathelicidin family — antimicrobial peptides that punch holes in bacterial membranes. It’s released by skin cells, white blood cells, and the gut lining; beyond killing germs, it summons immune cells and speeds wound healing. Too much of it is linked to rosacea flare-ups. As a therapy for wounds and infections, it’s research-stage — promising in animals, unproven in people. Scroll for the full picture.

01The peptide

What LL-37 actually is

Long before pharmacies, your body invented its own antibiotics. LL-37 is the human flagship — and unlike most peptides here, you make it every day.

Thirty-seven beads, all active: the whole chain folds into a helix whose two-faced chemistry wedges into microbial membranes and ruptures them.

Named like a license plate

LL = the first two amino acids (both leucine). 37 = the total length. Peptide naming is refreshingly literal.

Cut from a bigger guard

It’s sliced from hCAP18, a precursor protein stockpiled in white blood cells and skin — released and activated where microbes invade.

The only human cathelicidin

Mice have several; humans have exactly one. That makes it both precious and hard to study by comparison.

Plain English

Think of LL-37 as barbed wire your body unrolls wherever skin breaks or germs gather — it both blocks invaders and calls for backup.

03How it might work

How it’s supposed to work

For a drug candidate, the mechanism is unusually direct — it’s a weapon, not a whisper.

The proposed chain — deploy, pierce, summon, rebuild. A weapon with a foreman’s second job.
Physical killing

The helix wedges into bacterial membranes and bursts them — physical destruction, much harder for microbes to evolve around than a chemical trick.

The foreman role

Beyond killing, LL-37 chemically summons immune cells to the site — the same molecule fights and coordinates.

Rebuild mode

Skin cells migrate and multiply faster in its presence — the killer doubles as a repair crew chief.

Plain English

The chain: deploy → pierce → summon → rebuild. Kill the invaders, call the crew, fix the wall — one molecule, three jobs.

04Evidence check

What the science actually supports

The unusual case where the human data documents the downside and the animal data documents the promise.

Kills microbes (lab)potent and broad
Kills bacteria, fungi, and some viruses in dishes — the least disputed fact in the file.
Rosacea link (humans)real association
Human data exists — for the downside: excess abnormal LL-37 drives rosacea inflammation.
Wound healing (animals)consistent benefit
LL-37-deficient mice heal slowly; adding it back helps. Clean cause-and-effect — in mice.
Therapeutic trialsearly and stalled
Wound and infection applications have been explored in early research; none has completed the human trials a therapy needs.

The paradox

The human evidence is strongest for harm (rosacea), while the healing story is mostly animal. That’s the honest shape of the file.

Resistance-resistant?

Physical membrane-bursting is hard to evolve around — a genuine advantage over classic antibiotics, if it can be bottled safely.

Bottling is the hard part

A membrane-burster that also summons inflammation is genuinely hard to dose — the therapy problem in one sentence.

Plain English

Strong biology, thin therapy file. The molecule works; turning it into a drug is the unsolved part.

06Risks & unknowns

Risks & unknowns

A weapon your body already wields — which tells you exactly where the dangers are: dose, aim, and friendly fire.

Observed

The rosacea warning

Observed fact: excess or abnormally processed LL-37 drives skin inflammation in rosacea — the body’s own dose-response lesson.

Observed

No therapeutic safety data

Observed fact: as an administered drug, human safety data is minimal — it’s never been through full trials.

Theoretical

Killing isn’t selective

Theoretical: a membrane-burster doesn’t spare beneficial microbes — the microbiome could pay for the disinfection.

Theoretical

Inflammation amplifier

Theoretical: the same summoning signals that help wounds could worsen inflammatory conditions.

Unknown

Dosing a weapon

Unknown: the line between “enough to heal” and “enough to inflame” has never been mapped in people.

Unknown

Resistance, eventually

Unknown: whether widespread use would breed resistant microbes — the question that haunts every antimicrobial.

Plain English

Your body doses LL-37 locally, briefly, precisely. A drug version would dose it systemically, repeatedly, approximately — that’s the entire risk story.
