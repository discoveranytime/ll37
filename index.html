LL-37
the body’s antibiotic

Your skin and immune cells manufacture their own antibiotic — a 37-link peptide that shreds microbes and directs wound repair. Scientists are trying to turn it into a drug. Here is where that stands.

Quick read

LL-37 is the only human member of the cathelicidin family — antimicrobial peptides that punch holes in bacterial membranes. It’s released by skin cells, white blood cells, and the gut lining; beyond killing germs, it summons immune cells and speeds wound healing. Too much of it is linked to rosacea flare-ups. As a therapy for wounds and infections, it’s research-stage — promising in animals, unproven in people. Scroll for the full picture.

01The peptide

What LL-37 actually is

Long before pharmacies, your body invented its own antibiotics. LL-37 is the human flagship — and unlike most peptides here, you make it every day.

Thirty-seven beads, all active: the whole chain folds into a helix whose two-faced chemistry wedges into microbial membranes and ruptures them.

Named like a license plate

LL = the first two amino acids (both leucine). 37 = the total length. Peptide naming is refreshingly literal.

Cut from a bigger guard

It’s sliced from hCAP18, a precursor protein stockpiled in white blood cells and skin — released and activated where microbes invade.

The only human cathelicidin

Mice have several; humans have exactly one. That makes it both precious and hard to study by comparison.

hCAP18 sits inert until a protease cuts it — the cathelin shell is discarded and the 37-amino-acid LL-37 is set loose.
Plain English

Think of LL-37 as barbed wire your body unrolls wherever skin breaks or germs gather — it both blocks invaders and calls for backup.

05Risks & unknowns

Risks & unknowns

A weapon your body already wields — which tells you exactly where the dangers are: dose, aim, and friendly fire.

Observed

The rosacea warning

Observed fact: excess or abnormally processed LL-37 drives skin inflammation in rosacea — the body’s own dose-response lesson.

Observed

No therapeutic safety data

Observed fact: as an administered drug, human safety data is minimal — it’s never been through full trials.

Theoretical

Killing isn’t selective

Theoretical: a membrane-burster doesn’t spare beneficial microbes — the microbiome could pay for the disinfection.

Theoretical

Inflammation amplifier

Theoretical: the same summoning signals that help wounds could worsen inflammatory conditions.

Unknown

Dosing a weapon

Unknown: the line between “enough to heal” and “enough to inflame” has never been mapped in people.

Unknown

Resistance, eventually

Unknown: whether widespread use would breed resistant microbes — the question that haunts every antimicrobial.

Context decides: a balanced, local burst protects; excess release — or LL-37 tangled with stray DNA — tips the same molecule toward inflammation.
Plain English

Your body doses LL-37 locally, briefly, precisely. A drug version would dose it systemically, repeatedly, approximately — that’s the entire risk story.

✓ What the evidence supports

  • The established tier — lab-dish killing, immune recruitment, animal wound-healing: solid biology (full tiered breakdown in Chapter 07).
  • Your body already makes it — released at skin, wounds, and infection sites.
  • Vitamin D drives its production — a real, documented link.

✗ What it does NOT do (yet)

  • Proven therapy in humans — no completed trial has shown it works as a treatment.
  • Safe as an injection — zero systemic safety data in people.
  • Kill selectively — friendly microbes burst just as easily.
  • Resistance-proof — physical killing is hard to evade, not impossible.
  • Replace antibiotics — elegance of mechanism is not proof of efficacy.

Unresolved questions

The honest gaps, stated plainly:

  • Can it be formulated so it survives the body long enough to act?
  • Is there a dose window between “enough to heal” and “enough to inflame”?
  • Would topical use wreck the skin microbiome it’s meant to protect?
  • Why do some people overproduce it (rosacea) — and can that be switched off?
06Head to head

LL-37 vs the field

LL-37 is a weapon and a signal — not a repair crew, not a hormone. Here’s how it compares to its neighbors, on actual biology and actual evidence — never on generic “healing” claims.

LL-37 vs Thymosin Alpha-1

Target

LL-37 ruptures microbial membranes directly and recruits immune cells to the site. Thymosin Alpha-1 is an immune-signaling peptide — it nudges T-cells and dendritic cells through receptor signaling, with no direct microbe-killing job.

Evidence

LL-37: lab dishes and animal models; no human therapy data. Thymosin Alpha-1: a real human clinical history — studied in viral infections, hepatitis, oncology support, vaccine response, and sepsis trials.

Key difference

LL-37 is a weapon that also signals; Thymosin Alpha-1 is a signal with no weapon. Different jobs, different evidence universes.

LL-37 vs BPC-157

Target

BPC-157 is a gut-derived fragment studied for proposed tissue-repair and blood-vessel effects. LL-37 is a host-defense peptide whose day job is destroying microbes on contact.

Evidence

Both live in the same evidence neighborhood: animal and lab work only, zero proven human therapies. Neither has completed the trials a drug needs.

Key difference

BPC-157 is talked about as repair; LL-37 as defense. Neither has human proof — but LL-37’s killing mechanism is at least directly observable in a dish.

LL-37 vs TB-500

Target

TB-500 (a thymosin beta-4 fragment) binds actin and is studied for cell migration — cells crawling to close a gap. LL-37 punches holes in membranes — microbes bursting, not cells migrating.

Evidence

Both: preclinical only. TB-500’s parent molecule reached Phase 2 trials that stalled; LL-37 has no systemic human data at all.

Key difference

Migration vs destruction: completely different biology under the hood, the same evidence ceiling above it.

LL-37 vs conventional antibiotics

Target

Antibiotics jam specific bacterial machinery — cell-wall building, protein factories. LL-37 physically bursts membranes, which is theoretically harder for microbes to evolve around.

Evidence

Antibiotics: decades of proven human use, millions of lives. LL-37 as a therapy: none — the elegant mechanism has never survived the journey into a finished drug.

Key difference

LL-37’s approach is elegant; antibiotics’ is proven. Elegance isn’t efficacy — and antibiotics work alongside the microbiome problem LL-37 would share.

LL-37 vs other host-defense peptides

Target

Defensins are smaller, disulfide-locked peptides — molecular padlocks. LL-37 is the lone human cathelicidin: a straight alpha-helix, no locks, that lies down in membranes and breaks them.

Evidence

Both families: mostly lab and animal evidence. Defensins have slightly more human observational data (levels in disease), but no defensin is an approved drug either.

Key difference

Humans get several defensins and exactly one cathelicidin — LL-37 carries the whole family name alone. Same evidence tier, lonelier branch.

Plain English

Every rival here does a different job: signaling, repair, migration, jamming machinery, or membrane-busting. LL-37’s distinction isn’t stronger evidence — it’s the only one here that kills with physics instead of chemistry.

What the biology clearly shows

Solid, repeatable results — the least disputed parts of the LL-37 file.

  • Bursts microbes on contact — bacteria, fungi, and some viruses, demonstrated repeatedly in lab dishes.
  • Recruits immune cells — a chemical scent trail (chemotaxis) that calls backup to the breach.

  • Part of your routine defense — released by skin, neutrophils, and gut lining where microbes gather.

Think of it this way: your immune system has a built-in security guard who both wrestles intruders to the ground and radios for backup.

◠ PREDICTED / EMERGING — UNPROVEN IN PEOPLE

Where researchers think it could go

Promising directions — but none has survived a completed human trial.

  • Biofilm-busting inside the body — it loosens bacterial "slime cities" in lab dishes; whether that works in living tissue is unproven.
  • A weapon against resistant infections — physical membrane-bursting is hard to evolve around, which is exactly why scientists care. No proven human therapy yet.
  • A wound-healing drug — consistent animal results, but therapeutic research has been early and stalled.
  • A template for synthetic mimics — chemists are redesigning the helix to keep the killing and drop the inflammation. Still experimental chemistry, not medicine.

Think of it this way: we have a convincing blueprint for a fortress — but nobody has finished building it yet.

08Who it may suit

Who it may suit — and who it should not

LL-37 has no established medical indications as a supplement — there is no approved use to be "suited" for. This chapter maps where the research lives and where caution is warranted. This is general information, not personal medical advice.

May be relevant for

  • Wound-healing and infection researchers — the only people LL-37 is genuinely "for" today: the usable data lives in their labs.
  • People learning about innate immunity — LL-37 is the cleanest human example of the body's own antibiotic at work.
  • Anyone curious about the vitamin D link — a real, documented connection between sunlight, vitamin D, and antimicrobial defense.
  • Rosacea researchers — the clearest human evidence about LL-37 is the downside; studying it helps map the condition.

May not suit — extra caution

  • People with rosacea or chronic inflammatory skin conditions — excess LL-37 drives the very inflammation they would want less of.
  • Anyone pregnant, breastfeeding, or under 18 — zero safety data exists for these groups.
  • People with autoimmune or inflammatory conditions — the immune-summoning signals are theoretical amplifiers of inflammation.
  • Anyone considering gray-market "research" vials — unregulated, unverified contents, no systemic safety data in people.
  • People looking to replace antibiotics — elegance of mechanism is not proof of efficacy; never skip real care.

Think of it this way: a fire alarm is great for researchers studying fires — but you would not install one by wiring it to a blowtorch.

General information, not personal medical advice. There are no established indications for LL-37 as a supplement — nothing here recommends for or against any use. For any health decision, talk to a licensed clinician.

The scienceMembrane lysis is the rupturing of a cell's outer membrane — physical destruction, as opposed to chemical poisoning of the cell's innards.
